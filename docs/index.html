Evolving a network that learns to walk

NEAT evolves both the weights and the shape of a small neural network that drives Gymnasium's BipedalWalker. No gradients and no GPU: just a population of 200 networks on a 4-core laptop, mutating, competing and growing. Every champion has at least one hidden layer.

1Progress, stage by stage

Each clip shows the champion at that stage on its median run: of the 10 unseen terrains it was judged on, the one with the middle score. That makes it a typical run, not a lucky or unlucky one. Played at 2× speed. The score in the corner is the running reward on that terrain, and "avg" is the mean over all 10.

2Training curve

Fitness is the mean reward over 3 terrains per generation, shared by the whole population. An episode is cut short if the walker stops moving forward, so standing still scores about −9 while falling costs −100. The champion line is the re-check on 10 unseen terrains every 10 generations.

4How it works

  • NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies) mutates weights, adds connections and adds nodes. Similar networks are grouped into species so new structure gets time to prove itself.
  • Hidden layer guaranteed. Every network starts as 24 → 8 → 4 with no direct input-to-output links. Networks with no hidden node on an input-to-output path are penalised and can never become champion.
  • Fair fitness. All genomes in a generation run on the same 3 terrains, which change every generation. Raw environment reward, so scores compare directly with PPO and TD3.
  • Early stop. An episode ends when the hull moves less than 1 unit in 150 steps. That saves most of the simulation time, but it also makes standing still a tempting local optimum.
  • Hall of fame. Every 10 generations the top 5 are re-run on 10 unseen terrains with full episodes. Only that average picks the champion and the milestones.
  • Hardware. Intel i5-8250U (4 cores / 8 threads), no GPU, 4 worker processes at ~50 MB each, sharing the CPU with another training run.
